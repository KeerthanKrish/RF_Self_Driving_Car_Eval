# RL Algorithms for Driving

What the literature and practice actually favor for this domain, why, and — since you have hands-on TD-MPC2 experience from the SO-101 arm project — a direct comparison of how model-based RL fares against the dominant model-free approaches specifically in driving contexts.

## The headline finding: model-free still dominates, but the frontier has moved

Across the CARLA-focused literature, **more than 80% of published studies still use plain model-free methods** — DQN, PPO, or SAC — despite a decade of model-based RL progress elsewhere. But the *frontier* of driving-RL research (2023 onward) is increasingly model-based/world-model work, specifically because driving RL has two properties model-based methods are unusually good at addressing: expensive/risky rollouts (sample efficiency matters more than almost anywhere else in RL) and the need for lookahead/anticipation at intersections and merges (planning matters).

Source: [Comprehensive Review of RL for AD in CARLA (2509.08221)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.08221), [Kiran et al., Deep RL for Autonomous Driving: A Survey (2002.00444)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2002.00444).

## Model-free methods

### PPO (Proximal Policy Optimization)
The single most-used algorithm in driving RL — one survey found standard PPO usage at 73.1% among reviewed studies, with CARLA the dominant evaluation platform (50%) and urban driving/lane-changing the dominant tasks. PPO's appeal is exactly its "boring reliability": it's robust to hyperparameters, simple to implement relative to TRPO, and computationally efficient, via its clipped surrogate objective that prevents destructively large policy updates. Its documented weakness for driving specifically: it can get **trapped in a limited search space** during policy updates, restricting adaptability to new situations/alternative strategies — a real concern for a domain with a long tail of rare scenarios. Variants like LFPPO (Lévy-flight-enhanced PPO) report large gains (99% vs. 81% success rate for standard PPO in one CARLA study) by improving exploration specifically to counter this.

Sources: [PPO in AD (PMC11946678)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11946678/).

### SAC (Soft Actor-Critic)
The dominant choice for **continuous-control** driving tasks (steering/throttle), because its entropy-maximization objective gives materially better sample efficiency than PPO in continuous action spaces — a real advantage for driving, where the action space is inherently continuous (steering angle, throttle/brake). Numerous driving-specific variants exist to handle its edge cases:
- **PASAC** (Parameterized SAC) — for hybrid discrete-continuous action spaces (e.g., "change lane" as a discrete choice with a continuous execution parameter).
- **Distributional SAC (DSAC)** — combined with rule-guided exploration and safety-aware control for lane-changing.
- **Discrete SAC variants (ADSAC)** — adapting SAC's entropy-maximization ideas to discrete highway lane/speed decisions.
- Priority/experience-replay-augmented SAC variants for overtaking/lane-change tasks.

Sources: [SAC in lane-changing survey](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0950705125002606), [Rule-Guided Distributional SAC](https://doi.org/10.3390/vehicles8030058).

### DQN / Rainbow / Double/Dueling DQN
The natural fit when the action space is **discrete maneuvers** (lane-change decisions, meta-actions like highway-env's `{LANE_LEFT, IDLE, LANE_RIGHT, FASTER, SLOWER}`). Plain DQN suffers from well-known instability and Q-value overestimation in this setting — one lane-change study reported DQN causing "frequent collisions" during training without careful safety-reward design. Double DQN and Dueling architectures directly address overestimation and state/advantage decomposition respectively. **Rainbow DQN combined with a safety-driven reward scheme achieved much higher sample efficiency** in one lane-change study — converging in ~200k steps vs. ~1M for a baseline approach. A common practical pattern is a **hierarchical DQN**: a discrete high-level decision layer (change lane or not) plus a lower control layer (adjustment/execution).

Sources: [DQN-based DRL for AD](https://www.academia.edu/146005948/DQN_Based_Deep_Reinforcement_Learning_for_Autonomous_Driving), [Lane-change with DRL (1904.10171)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1904.10171).

## Model-based RL / world models — the direct comparison you asked about

This is the area most relevant to your existing TD-MPC2 background, so it gets extended treatment.

### The core idea and why it fits driving well
Model-based RL learns a **dynamics model** (and typically also a reward model and termination model) of the environment, then either plans directly against that learned model (MPC-style, as TD-MPC2 does) or trains a policy inside *imagined* rollouts generated by the model (as the Dreamer family does), rather than requiring every gradient update to come from a real environment step. For driving, this directly attacks two of the domain's worst properties for model-free RL:

1. **Sample/rollout cost.** Every real environment step in a photorealistic simulator (CARLA) is expensive to render, and every real-world step (on physical hardware) is expensive and risky to collect. A world model lets most of the "experience" an agent learns from be imagined rather than actually simulated/driven.
2. **Anticipation/lookahead.** Deciding whether to accept a gap at an intersection, or whether to start braking now for a light that might turn red, benefits from short-horizon forward planning — which is what MPC-style model-based methods do explicitly, and what pure model-free value/policy learning does only implicitly (baked into a learned value function with no explicit lookahead).

### DreamerV3 / Dreamer-family in driving — currently the more mature line of work
DreamerV3 has become something close to a **foundational algorithm for world-model-based driving research**:
- **CarDreamer** is an open-source platform that packages DreamerV2, DreamerV3, and Plan2Explore behind a standardized Gym interface specifically for CARLA-based tasks.
- **Think2Drive** (ECCV 2024) is the most prominent concrete result: it trains a compact latent world model (learning the environment's transition, reward, and termination dynamics) using DreamerV3 as the base algorithm, with a **Bird's-Eye-View (BEV) input representation** precomputed and cached for efficiency, and a planner model trained to maximize reward predicted *by the world model itself*. It's evaluated on the official CARLA Leaderboard v1 and v2 test routes, with especially strong relative performance on the more complex v2 routes (which include the 39 curated corner cases). One description of the philosophy captures the paradigm shift well: *"Learn a compact world. Then learn to act inside that world. Only after mastering the dream should you step into reality... CARLA becomes the final exam, not the training ground."* This is a genuinely different framing from "run PPO against the simulator for a million steps."
- Other recent work: **InDRiVE** (reward-free world-model pretraining) reports comparable lane-following performance to task-trained DreamerV3 with *better* generalization to a held-out town and higher collision-avoidance success under town shift, suggesting the world-model itself — even without task-specific reward — captures transferable structure.
- **AdaWM** explores adaptive world-model-based planning specifically to handle distribution shift in driving.

### TD-MPC2 in driving — real but comparatively sparse, and a genuine DIY-integration gap
Unlike Dreamer, **TD-MPC2 does not yet have a mature, off-the-shelf driving-simulator integration** the way it has for DMControl, Meta-World, or ManiSkill (the benchmark suites its own published results use). What exists:
- TD-MPC2 itself is a general continuous-control algorithm (latent trajectory planning + SimNorm normalization + TD-learning combined with MPC-style planning) shown to scale to 104 diverse tasks with one hyperparameter set — a strong generalist result, but not driving-specific by default.
- A blog-post-level experiment, ["Driving With Imagination: DreamerV3 + TD-MPC in CARLA"](https://satyamcser.substack.com/p/driving-with-imagination-dreamerv3), directly compares the two model-based approaches in a CARLA setting — one of the few places this comparison has actually been run.
- TD-MPC2 was also evaluated (alongside PPO and SAC) in a recent **"Reset-Free Reinforcement Learning for Real-World Agile Driving"** empirical study on small-scale real hardware — directly relevant because it's exactly the kind of "every rollout is physically costly" setting where your TD-MPC2 experience with sample efficiency should transfer most cleanly.
- Practically: if you want to use TD-MPC2 for this project, expect to do the same kind of integration work you likely already did connecting TD-MPC2 to the SO-101 arm environment — there's no plug-and-play `tdmpc2 + highway-env` or `tdmpc2 + MetaDrive` pipeline waiting for you the way there is for SB3 + PPO/SAC.

### MuZero and lineage
Discrete MuZero has been shown to **match PPO's peak performance with much higher data efficiency** in driving-relevant settings, and **EfficientZero** improves sample efficiency further on top of MuZero. A 2024 survey (["World Models for Autonomous Driving: An Initial Survey"](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2403.02622)) traces the broader lineage from PlaNet through the Dreamer family, MuZero, and on to large-scale video-generation-flavored world models (Sora, Cosmos, Genie), framing autonomous driving as one of several domains (alongside robotics and video generation) now converging on world-model approaches.

### Practical comparison for your decision
| | Model-free (PPO/SAC/DQN) | Model-based (Dreamer-family / TD-MPC2 / MuZero) |
|---|---|---|
| Tooling maturity for driving | High — Stable-Baselines3 + CARLA/MetaDrive/highway-env wrappers all exist and are documented | Medium (Dreamer, via CarDreamer/Think2Drive) to low (TD-MPC2 — DIY integration expected) |
| Sample efficiency | Lower — needs many real/simulated env steps | Much higher — most learning happens on imagined rollouts |
| Implementation complexity | Lower | Higher — must jointly learn dynamics/reward/termination models, tune planning horizon, etc. |
| Fit for real-world/physical deployment later | Weaker (every real rollout is expensive) | Stronger — this is precisely the setting model-based RL is designed to help with |
| Published driving-specific track record | Extensive (>80% of literature) | Smaller but fast-growing and increasingly state-of-the-art on the hardest CARLA benchmarks (Think2Drive on Leaderboard v2) |

**Bottom line**: model-free (PPO/SAC) remains the pragmatic default for getting something working quickly with mature tooling, especially at smaller project scopes (see `06_recommendations.md`). Model-based/world-model RL is where the state-of-the-art on the *hardest* driving benchmarks currently sits, and is specifically worth reaching for once sample efficiency starts to bind (larger maps, real-hardware ambitions, or simply wanting to reuse your existing TD-MPC2 expertise) — but budget real integration effort, especially for TD-MPC2 specifically, since Dreamer-family tooling for driving is considerably further along.

## Imitation learning + RL hybrids

**Behavior cloning (BC)** directly learns observation→action mappings from expert demonstrations but suffers from **compounding error**: small mistakes push the agent into states the expert demonstrations never covered, and error accumulates. **DAgger** (Dataset Aggregation) is the standard fix: iteratively execute the *current* learned policy, have the expert relabel the states it actually visited, and retrain — this makes the training distribution match the deployment distribution. A common and very practical pattern in real systems is **BC-as-warm-start**: initialize a policy via BC, then either continue with DAgger or switch to RL fine-tuning from that warm start rather than from scratch. Baidu's **ApolloRL** is a concrete industrial example of exactly this pattern: pretrain via supervised learning on expert driver logs, then RL-refine in short (20–30 second) scenario slices — with the caveat, reported directly by the researchers, that the resulting agent still could not directly replace the production on-road system or an experienced human driver.

**Safe DAgger** extends this further for safety: it trains an additional learned **"safety policy"** that predicts (without querying the expensive/risky reference expert) whether the primary learned policy is likely to deviate dangerously from the expert in the current state, and only queries/hands control to the real expert/reference when that safety policy flags risk. This minimizes costly or dangerous reference-policy queries during both training and deployment — a genuinely useful pattern to know about if you ever move toward real-hardware deployment.

Sources: [ApolloRL](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2201.12609), [Safe DAgger / Query-Efficient Imitation Learning](https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/AAAI/article/view/10857/10716), [Interactive and Hybrid Imitation Learning](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2412.07057).

## Offline RL

Motivated by a hard constraint: **online exploration in real traffic is unsafe and often impractical**, so offline RL learns a policy purely from a fixed, previously-collected dataset with no further environment interaction. **Conservative Q-Learning (CQL)** is the dominant method here — it adds a penalty term that explicitly suppresses Q-values for out-of-distribution (unseen-in-the-dataset) state-action pairs while raising Q-values for actions actually present in the data, enforcing a conservative lower bound that avoids the extrapolation/overestimation errors that plague naive offline value learning. Applying CQL with entity-centric transformer state representations on the Waymo Open Motion Dataset produced a **3.1× improvement in success rate and 7.6× reduction in collision rate** relative to the strongest behavior-cloning baseline in one study — a large, concrete gap illustrating why offline RL is preferred over plain BC when a static logged dataset is all you have. F1TENTH racing has its own dedicated offline-RL literature for exactly the same "can't safely explore live" reason.

Sources: [Offline RL for AD comparative study (2508.07029)](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.07029), [CQL paper](https://papers.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2020/file/0d2b2061826a5df3221116a5085a6052-Paper.pdf), [F1tenth Offline RL (2408.04198)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.04198).

## Multi-agent RL (relevant if surrounding traffic is itself learning agents)

If other cars in your city are learning agents rather than scripted, you inherit the full complexity of multi-agent RL: **non-stationarity** (the environment looks non-stationary from any single agent's perspective because every other agent's policy is simultaneously changing), scalability challenges, and safety. Two comprehensive recent surveys map this space in detail: ["Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning in Intelligent Transportation Systems"](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.20315v2) (covering both traffic-signal control and driving) and ["Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning for Autonomous Driving: A Survey"](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.09675) (proposing simulator-evaluation metrics and surveying environment modeling/state representation/perception/algorithm design choices).

A genuinely important empirical finding for anyone considering this design choice: **pure self-play does not automatically produce human-like or even safe traffic.** ["Human-compatible driving partners through data-regularized self-play reinforcement learning"](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.19648) reports that naive large-scale self-play drifts toward unrealistic, non-human equilibria (agents that maximize reward against each other but behave unpredictably around actual humans). Their fix — **Human-Regularized PPO (HR-PPO)** — adds a small penalty for deviating from a logged human-driving reference policy during self-play training, and achieves a 93% success rate with 3.5% off-road rate and 3% collision rate while remaining measurably closer to real human driving-log statistics. The practical takeaway: if you want other learning cars in your city, plan for **anchoring self-play to logged human data from the start**, not naive self-play — SMARTS' "zoo of diverse pretrained policies" approach and log-replay/reactive-agent hybrids (as in nuPlan and Waymax) are alternative ways to get the same "not just self-play into an alien equilibrium" benefit.

Sources: [MARL for ITS survey (2508.20315)](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.20315v2), [MARL for AD survey (2408.09675)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.09675), [HR-PPO / Human-compatible driving partners (2403.19648)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.19648).

## Hierarchical RL

Given a driving task naturally decomposes into "what should I do" (route/maneuver-level intent) and "how do I do it smoothly" (low-level trajectory tracking), **Hierarchical RL (HRL)** is a recurring and empirically successful pattern. The **options framework** (Sutton et al.) provides the formal basis: an option is a temporally-extended action with an initiation set, an internal policy, and a termination condition. A common concrete architecture in the driving literature is **three levels**: a high-level intention/maneuver planner, a mid-level trajectory/waypoint planner, and a low-level PID (or similar) controller that tracks the generated trajectory. One CARLA-based study reported this structure **reduces convergence time, generates smoother trajectories, and better handles dynamic surroundings and noisy observations** compared to flat single-level RL. Other concrete applications: option-policy-driven HRL specifically for the overtaking maneuver, and hierarchical structures for intersection navigation. This is also directly relevant to the long-horizon credit-assignment problem discussed in `05_challenges_and_pitfalls.md` — temporal abstraction shortens the effective decision horizon the top-level policy has to reason over.

Sources: [Hierarchical RL for AD survey material](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2111.05479), [Trajectory Planning via HRL (CMU)](https://publications.ri.cmu.edu/trajectory-planning-for-autonomous-vehicles-using-hierarchical-reinforcement-learning), [HRL for intersection navigation (KDD)](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3580305.3599839).

## What the literature actually favors, in one paragraph

For **decision/control given a reasonably good state representation**, PPO and SAC remain the pragmatic workhorses, with SAC preferred whenever the action space is continuous and sample efficiency matters even a little. For **discrete maneuver-level decisions**, DQN/Rainbow variants remain common and effective, especially paired with explicit safety-reward shaping. The **frontier** — and the place your existing model-based RL background is most differentiating — is Dreamer-family world models (mature, CARLA-integrated tooling exists) and, with more DIY integration effort, TD-MPC2-style MPC planning, both motivated by the same core insight: driving rollouts (in a photorealistic sim, and especially on real hardware) are too expensive to be sample-inefficient about. Almost every *serious, large-scale* system in practice layers imitation learning underneath RL rather than training RL from a blank slate, and offline RL is the standard response whenever "just go explore live" is unsafe — which, in real driving, is most of the time.
