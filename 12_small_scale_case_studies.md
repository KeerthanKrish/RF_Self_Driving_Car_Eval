# Small-Scale Case Studies: What Individuals and Small Teams Have Actually Built

Every earlier file in this dossier draws overwhelmingly on lab-scale research — university groups, industrial AV teams, well-funded benchmarks. This file deliberately inverts that lens: what have solo developers, students, and small teams actually shipped and shared, without a research lab's compute budget or headcount? The goal is an honest, grounded sense of what's achievable at your actual likely scale, as a direct counterweight to the rest of this dossier.

## The standout solo project: Antonin Raffin's "Learning to Drive Smoothly in Minutes"

If there's one project on this entire research pass worth reading start to finish as a model for how to run a small, well-scoped driving-RL project, it's this one. [`araffin/learning-to-drive-in-5-minutes`](https://github.com/araffin/learning-to-drive-in-5-minutes) (Antonin Raffin, with Roma Sokolkov, 2019) trains a Donkey Car simulator agent to drive smoothly using **only a single forward-facing camera**, in about **five minutes of actual interaction time** — the headline claim the project is named after, and a genuinely dramatic sample-efficiency number for a from-camera-pixels RL task.

**How it actually works, and why it's instructive**: rather than feeding raw camera pixels directly into an RL algorithm's policy network (slow, high-variance, as `03`'s sensing-modality discussion would predict), the project first trains a **Variational Autoencoder (VAE)** to compress each camera frame into a compact 64-dimensional latent vector, offline and separately from the RL training itself. That latent vector, concatenated with a short history of the last ten steering/throttle actions (giving an 84-dimensional feature vector total), is what the actual control policy — a small two-layer (32 then 16 units) fully-connected network — receives as input. The RL algorithm itself is **SAC** (Soft Actor-Critic), chosen specifically for its sample efficiency in continuous control, exactly matching `02`'s general recommendation. The five-minute training time is a direct consequence of this pipeline: by the time SAC starts training, the genuinely hard perception problem (raw pixels → compact useful representation) has already been solved by the VAE, so the RL algorithm is only ever solving the comparatively easy problem of mapping an 84-dimensional vector to smooth steering/throttle commands.

This project is also notable for its downstream influence rather than just its own result: Antonin Raffin went on to become a core maintainer of Stable-Baselines3 itself (`08`), and this project's VAE-then-RL pattern reappears, essentially unchanged in spirit, in `idreesshaikh`'s currently-maintained CARLA repo (`07`) — a genuine example of a hobbyist-scale technique propagating into later, larger projects rather than staying a one-off curiosity.

**What this means for your project**: the lesson isn't "use a VAE" specifically — it's that **a well-chosen representation-learning step can matter more than algorithm sophistication** for getting a tractable RL problem out of a raw-sensor driving task, and that this is achievable by one or two people in a genuinely short amount of both wall-clock and engineering time when the scope (a single Donkey Car track, not a city) is chosen deliberately narrow.

Sources: [Learning to Drive Smoothly in Minutes (blog)](https://araffin.github.io/project/learning-to-drive/), [Medium writeup](https://medium.com/data-science/learning-to-drive-smoothly-in-minutes-450a7cdb35f4), [GitHub repo](https://github.com/araffin/learning-to-drive-in-5-minutes).

## Other solo/small-team repos, with an honest read on scale and currency

- [`idreesshaikh/Autonomous-Driving-in-Carla-using-Deep-Reinforcement-Learning`](https://github.com/idreesshaikh/Autonomous-Driving-in-Carla-using-Deep-Reinforcement-Learning) (563 stars, commits as recently as Nov 2025) — PPO + VAE in CARLA, the same core pattern as Raffin's project applied to a much heavier simulator. The most currently-alive solo CARLA+RL project found in this research pass, and a reasonable existence proof that the "narrow scope + mature RL library + representation-learning trick" formula scales up from Donkey Car to CARLA, at real (if reduced, relative to a lab) cost in engineering time.
- [`bitsauce/Carla-ppo`](https://github.com/bitsauce/Carla-ppo) (268 stars, last active 2021) — explicitly built, per its own README framing, to make CARLA RL more accessible to people without a research lab's resources; a genuinely solid single-developer engineering effort, now stale but still a readable reference for the gym-wrapper pattern.
- [`flyyufelix/donkey_rl`](https://github.com/flyyufelix/donkey_rl) (133 stars, 2018) — the original DDQN-in-Unity-Donkey-Car project and blog post, predating Raffin's SAC-based project by about a year; worth reading as the "first attempt" this later, more refined project improved on.
- [`songyanho/Reinforcement-Learning-for-Self-Driving-Cars`](https://github.com/songyanho/Reinforcement-Learning-for-Self-Driving-Cars) (155 stars, 2020) — a solid mid-scale student/hobbyist project, speed-maximization framing, reasonable code quality, unmaintained since 2020.
- [`JuliaSokolova/DeepRacer`](https://github.com/JuliaSokolova/DeepRacer) (1 star) — a Galvanize data-science-bootcamp capstone project using AWS DeepRacer. Included deliberately *despite* its tiny star count: it's an honest data point that even a short-format bootcamp capstone (not a multi-year PhD project) can produce a real, working, documented RL driving result — the ceiling isn't only reachable by people with years of ML background, though the polish gap between this and Raffin's project is also real and worth being honest about.

## Competitions — the realistic way to get external validation without a lab's budget

### AWS DeepRacer
A **1/18th-scale physical (or purely virtual) racing car** platform with a genuinely structured competitive league: **Time Trial** (fastest lap, no traffic), **Object Avoidance** (dodge static obstacles), and **Head-to-Head** (race against other cars, elimination-bracket format), split into **Open** and **Pro** skill divisions, plus a dedicated **Student League**. The entire skill expression in DeepRacer is **reward function engineering** — the RL algorithm itself is fixed/managed by the platform, so competitive success comes from crafting a reward function that shapes smooth, fast, track-following behavior, directly exercising the same reward-design skills covered in `04` (including its exact failure modes — a DeepRacer reward function that over-rewards raw speed without properly weighting track-following is a smaller-scale, hands-on instance of the same "proxy reward vs. true objective" gap `04` and `05` describe). Scope-wise, this is racing-loop, single-agent (in Time Trial) or simple-adversarial (Head-to-Head) — not urban driving with intersections/traffic-rules/pedestrians — but it is genuinely the lowest-friction way for an individual to train a driving-RL policy and get it ranked against a real global community, without needing your own simulator, compute cluster, or dataset.

A concrete small-team result: a team from Esade's master's program reported a **top 1% ranking** in a DeepRacer-based competition as an explicit part of a course capstone project — a credible existence proof that competitive results are reachable without a research lab, on a course-project timescale.

### DIYRobocars — grassroots, mostly behavior cloning, RL genuinely rare
DIYRobocars runs quarterly grassroots races (reported to draw hundreds of spectators and a dozen-plus competing cars) built around Donkey Car and similar small RC-scale platforms. The honest finding from this research pass: **the large majority of DIYRobocars competitors use behavior cloning (train a CNN on recorded expert-driving camera frames), not RL** — for exactly the reason `05` predicts: live RL exploration on real hardware means the car crashes repeatedly while learning, which is both slow and physically costly in a way that BC's "record an expert lap, then train offline" workflow simply avoids. [`downingbots/RLDonkeycar`](https://github.com/downingbots/RLDonkeycar) (real-time RL for a physical Donkey Car using two Raspberry Pis) is one of the genuinely rare examples of someone actually attempting live RL on real hardware in this community rather than in simulation first — worth reading specifically as a case study in what that harder path actually requires, rather than as evidence it's the community norm.

### NeurIPS Driving SMARTS (2022)
Built on the SMARTS platform (`01`), this competition drew **87 participants across 53 teams** — a real, moderate-scale (not Kaggle-flagship-scale, but genuinely substantial) academic competition specifically on distribution-shift-robust driving RL/offline-learning, with a two-track structure explicitly supporting both RL and offline-learning submissions, and curated scenarios spanning difficulty from simple cruising to unprotected turns at unsignalized intersections. This is realistic evidence that a mid-size, focused competition on exactly this topic exists and is enterable — a useful target if external, comparative validation of your own work (rather than DeepRacer's racing-only scope) is something you'd want later.

Sources: [AWS DeepRacer League](https://aws.amazon.com/deepracer/), [Esade DeepRacer capstone](https://dobetter.esade.edu/en/reinforcement-learning), [downingbots/RLDonkeycar](https://github.com/downingbots/RLDonkeycar), [NeurIPS 2022 Competition: Driving SMARTS (2211.07545)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.07545), [Driving SMARTS competition outcomes](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v220/rasouli23a.html).

- **CMU's Robotics Institute MSCV program** ran a team capstone project training a CARLA-based RL driving agent (`mscvprojects.ri.cmu.edu/2020teamd`) — a useful reference for "what a funded but still student-scale, single-semester team project looks like" in this exact space, sitting between a solo hobbyist repo and a full lab publication in scope.

## The honest pattern, synthesized

A few things are consistent enough across every example in this file to be worth stating as direct takeaways for scoping your own project:

1. **Every well-documented, genuinely reusable small-team success here stayed narrow in scope** — a single race track (Donkey Car, DeepRacer), a single simple town (the more modest CARLA repos), never a full city. This directly reinforces `06`'s Tier 1/Tier 2 framing: the solo/small-team success stories in the wild overwhelmingly live at exactly those scopes, not Tier 3.
2. **Nobody rewrote an RL algorithm from scratch.** Every credible small-team project here builds on an existing library (SB3 above all, occasionally a platform-managed algorithm as in DeepRacer) — the engineering effort goes into environment wrapping, observation representation (the VAE pattern), and reward design, not into re-deriving PPO or SAC.
3. **RL specifically, on real (not simulated) hardware, remains a small, effortful niche even within the hobbyist community** — DIYRobocars' behavior-cloning dominance is the clearest evidence of this, and it's not an accident: it's the same safety/sample-cost tension `05` describes, just encountered firsthand by hobbyists with an RC car budget rather than by researchers with a safety team. If real-hardware deployment is ever a goal for this project, expect to be in a much smaller, harder-earned category of prior work (`downingbots/RLDonkeycar`, F1TENTH/RoboRacer's offline-RL racing literature from `01`/`02`) than if you stay in simulation.
4. **Competitions are the most realistic path to external, comparative validation without lab-scale resources** — AWS DeepRacer for a low-friction on-ramp (racing-scope, but genuinely global and structured), NeurIPS Driving SMARTS for a closer match to this project's actual subject matter (urban driving decisions, distribution shift) at a real if modest participation scale.
